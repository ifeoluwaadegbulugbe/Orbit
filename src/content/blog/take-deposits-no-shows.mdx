## Why no-shows keep happening without a deposit

Without a deposit, a booking costs the client nothing to cancel, even at the last minute. There's no financial consequence to simply not showing up, so for some clients, a lower-priority appointment quietly gets skipped the moment something better comes along. You're left with an empty slot you can't fill on short notice, and no way to recover the lost income.

A deposit changes the math. It turns a booking from a free, no-consequence reservation into a small financial commitment, which is usually enough to make a client think twice before canceling without notice, or at least call ahead instead of just not showing up.

## Setting a deposit amount that actually works

The goal of a deposit isn't to profit from cancellations, it's to make a no-show cost the client something real. Too small, and it won't change behavior. Too large, and it can discourage new clients from booking at all.

A reasonable starting point is 20 to 50 percent of the total service cost, scaled to how much a no-show actually costs you. A high-value appointment, like a bridal trial or a multi-hour project, justifies a larger deposit than a quick 30-minute touch-up, simply because the lost slot is worth more.

Whatever number you land on, apply it consistently. A deposit policy that changes client to client, with no clear logic, feels arbitrary and erodes trust faster than having a deposit at all.

## Writing a deposit policy clients will actually read

A deposit policy only works if clients know about it before they book, not after they've already cancelled. Keep it short and specific:

> "A deposit of [amount] is required to confirm your appointment. Deposits are non-refundable for cancellations with less than 24 hours' notice, but fully transferable if you reschedule with more notice than that."

State the amount, the cancellation window, and what happens if that window is missed. Avoid vague language like "deposits may be forfeited" without defining the actual timeline, since vague policies lead to disputes exactly when you can least afford one.

## Where to put the policy so it's actually seen

The policy needs to appear at the moment a client is deciding to book, not buried somewhere they'll never scroll to. Include it directly on your [booking page](/product/booking), and repeat the key line again when you request the deposit itself, so there's no later claim that they weren't told.

If you're taking deposits manually over WhatsApp, paste the policy as text before sending a payment request, every single time, not just for clients you suspect might cancel. Consistency here is what makes the policy enforceable; selectively applying it to "risky" clients and not others invites complaints about unfair treatment.

## Making it easy to actually pay the deposit

A deposit policy only works if paying it is frictionless. If a client has to navigate a complicated bank transfer process just to secure a slot, some will simply give up and book somewhere else instead.

Attaching a direct payment link to the booking removes that friction. ## Handling the awkward cancellation conversation

Enforcing a deposit policy occasionally means telling a client their deposit isn't refundable, which can feel uncomfortable, especially with regulars. The key is to have decided the policy in advance, in writing, so you're pointing to an existing rule rather than making a judgment call on the spot.

A message like, "I totally understand things come up. Per our policy, deposits within 24 hours of the appointment aren't refundable, but I'd be glad to apply it toward your next booking if you'd like to reschedule for later this week," is firm without being harsh. It also offers a path forward, which most clients appreciate more than a flat refusal.

## What a deposit doesn't fix

A deposit reduces no-shows; it doesn't eliminate them entirely, and it won't fix a scheduling system that's confusing or hard to use in the first place. If clients are unclear about their appointment time because reminders aren't going out, a deposit won't solve that underlying problem.

Pair a deposit policy with an automatic reminder the day before an appointment. Most no-shows aren't malicious, they're genuinely forgotten. A reminder catches the honest mistakes; a deposit discourages the deliberate ones. ## Start small and adjust

If you've never charged a deposit before, start with a modest amount and a clear policy, then adjust based on what you see. If no-shows persist at the same rate, the deposit may be too low relative to the cost of the service. If new bookings drop off noticeably, it may be set too high for your market. Either way, a deposit policy you can measure and adjust beats no policy at all.
